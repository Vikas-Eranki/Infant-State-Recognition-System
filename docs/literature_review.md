# Literature Review: Infant State Recognition System

---

## Section 1 — Introduction and Motivation

Infant crying is the sole pre-linguistic channel through which neonates communicate physiological and psychological distress to caregivers. Unlike adult speech, which encodes meaning through lexical and syntactic structure, infant vocalizations carry their entire semantic content — hunger, pain, discomfort, loneliness, fear — within their spectro-temporal acoustic patterns alone. Since cry signals carry information about a baby's wellbeing and can be understood by experienced parents and experts to an extent, recognition and analysis of an infant's cry is not only possible but also has profound medical and societal applications [3]. Despite this clinical significance, the predominant response to infant crying remains entirely manual: caregivers develop interpretive intuition through prolonged exposure, an ability that is neither transferable, scalable, nor reliably available to first-time parents during nocturnal care episodes when fatigue impairs judgment most severely.

The consequences of misinterpreting infant distress signals extend beyond caregiver frustration. Delayed feeding response in neonates contributes to hypoglycemic episodes during critical developmental windows. Failure to distinguish pain vocalizations from hunger cries can mask early indicators of gastrointestinal distress, infection, or neurological anomaly. Differentiating between cries is very challenging; for instance, the difference between a sleepy cry and a sick cry can be difficult for caregivers, and because of these reasons a system that can solve this problem efficiently needs to be developed [3]. Commercial baby monitor solutions address only cry *detection* — determining whether a cry event is occurring — without attempting the harder and clinically more valuable problem of cry *reason classification*: determining why the infant is crying. This gap between detection and classification defines the central unsolved problem in automated infant care technology.

The present work addresses this gap through the design and evaluation of a multi-class Infant State Recognition System capable of distinguishing eight distinct cry categories — hunger, belly pain, burping, discomfort, tiredness, loneliness, cold/hot, and fear — from raw audio signals. The system is structured as a three-tier comparative architecture: an Advanced Machine Learning baseline using Support Vector Machines on handcrafted spectral features, a Deep Learning model using a Convolutional Neural Network on Log-Mel Spectrogram representations, and a Hybrid system that fuses both approaches to demonstrate performance gains beyond either component alone. Abbaskhah et al. (2023) [2] demonstrated that CNN and MLP models outperform SVM on identical MFCC feature inputs due to their greater representational complexity — a finding that directly motivates the comparative ablation structure of the present system. The remainder of this document reviews the three bodies of literature that ground each tier of this architecture, identifies the specific gaps each tier addresses, and positions the present work within the trajectory of the field.

## Section 2 — Historical Development of Infant Cry Analysis

Systematic scientific inquiry into infant vocalizations began not with machine learning but with the trained human ear. Research on infant cry started as early as the 1960s when the Wasz-Höckert research group [5] identified four types of cries — pain, hunger, birth, and pleasure — auditorily by trained nurses, with early research determining that different types of cries can be differentiated auditorily by trained adult listeners. This foundational observation — that cry categories carry acoustically distinguishable signatures perceivable by experienced listeners — established the scientific premise that automated recognition was theoretically achievable. However, human auditory discrimination is subjective, fatigue-dependent, and impossible to scale. Training humans to perceive infant cries is more challenging than training machine learning models, as human perception is subjective and prone to bias, whereas machine learning models can consistently analyze large datasets and identify subtle patterns that may be missed by human listeners [3]. The computational treatment of infant cry analysis, emerging in the 1990s, was therefore motivated not merely by convenience but by a fundamental limitation of the human perceptual baseline.

The first computational era of infant cry analysis was dominated by hand-engineered acoustic representations and classical statistical classifiers. Liu et al. (2019) [1] obtained and analyzed audio features of infant cry signals in both time and frequency domains, extracting Linear Predictive Coding (LPC), Linear Predictive Cepstral Coefficients (LPCC), Bark Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (BFCC), and Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) as the feature space for cry language recognition. This feature extraction paradigm — converting raw waveforms into compact cepstral representations that model the spectral envelope of sound production — drew directly from the speech processing literature, treating the infant larynx as a source-filter system analogous to the adult vocal tract. The theoretical appeal of MFCC in this context is well-founded: the mel scale compresses high-frequency detail while preserving perceptually relevant low-frequency energy, and the cepstral transformation decorrelates filterbank coefficients, making them well-suited for classifiers that assume feature independence such as Gaussian Mixture Models and Naive Bayes. According to Liu et al. (2019) [1], cry audio waveform and time-frequency analysis are conducted to extract features such as MFCC and Bark Frequency Cepstral Coefficients, with the final step of cry recognition formulating features with a vertical vector and obtaining sparse representation using compressive sensing.

The Support Vector Machine emerged during this era as the consistently dominant classifier for infant cry tasks. MFCC feature extraction applied to infant cry classification with decision tree, Naive Bayes, and Support Vector Machine classifiers demonstrates that SVM achieves the highest performance metrics in both accuracy and F1-score — results obtained from 11 MFCC features, confirming MFCC as a suitable feature representation for infant cry classification [2]. The theoretical advantage of SVM in this setting stems from its maximum-margin formulation: by finding the hyperplane that maximizes the separation between class boundaries in a high-dimensional kernel-projected feature space, SVM produces classifiers that generalize well on small datasets — a persistent characteristic of infant cry corpora where per-class sample counts rarely exceed a few hundred recordings. The RBF kernel, in particular, allows non-linear decision boundaries without explicitly computing the transformed feature space, through the identity K(x, y) = exp(−γ||x − y||²), where γ controls the influence radius of individual training samples.

The construction of crowdsourced, multi-reason datasets marked the next inflection point in the field. Prior to 2015, the majority of infant cry research operated on pathologically-labelled corpora distinguishing sick from healthy newborns — a clinically valuable but narrow framing. The Donate-A-Cry campaign by Veres (2015) [4] fundamentally expanded the problem scope by collecting cry recordings from parents worldwide across nine reason categories encoding hunger, burping, belly pain, discomfort, tiredness, loneliness, cold/hot, fear, and unknown states, with infant gender and age encoded directly in audio filenames. Infant cry audio has a fundamental frequency typically ranging from 400 to 600 Hz, with harmonic content extending to 4000 Hz and above [10]. Liang et al. (2022) [10] emphasize that stable spectral features are obtained by cutting the signal into smaller parts, then applying the Hamming window, Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), Mel filter, and discrete cosine transform (DCT) to obtain the MFCC signal — a pipeline that captures this full spectral range rather than the fundamental frequency alone. This crowdsourced corpus, and the Baby Cry Sense dataset derived from it, represents the most ecologically valid available dataset for real-world cry reason classification — capturing the acoustic messiness of home environments, mobile recording devices, and unsupervised labelling by non-expert parents. It is precisely this dataset, with its inherent class imbalance and dual-population sampling rate artifact identified in the present work's EDA, that forms the experimental foundation of the system described herein.

## Section 3 — Machine Learning Approaches: Capabilities and Limitations

The application of classical machine learning to infant cry classification reached its maturity through a consistent pipeline: extract handcrafted acoustic features from raw audio, construct a fixed-dimensional feature vector per sample, and train a discriminative classifier on those vectors. Together with traditional machine learning classifiers such as KNN, SVM, and GMM, features such as MFCC, spectrogram, and fundamental frequency extracted from different domains can discriminate frame-based signals from one another and be used to train machine learning classifiers [9]. Among these classifiers, the Support Vector Machine consistently demonstrated superior generalization on infant cry corpora — a result attributable to its maximum-margin formulation, which finds the decision hyperplane maximizing the separation between class boundaries in a kernel-projected feature space. This property is particularly advantageous in the infant cry domain, where per-class sample counts rarely exceed a few hundred recordings and the risk of overfitting is therefore severe.

Liu et al. (2019) [1] represent the state of this paradigm at its most rigorous. By extracting LPC, LPCC, BFCC, and MFCC features in both time and frequency domains and formulating features as vertical vectors with sparse representation obtained using compressive sensing, the system achieves cry language recognition even under noisy conditions. The compressive sensing component is particularly notable: by exploiting the sparsity of cry feature representations in a transformed basis, the system recovers discriminative structure from degraded recordings without requiring clean studio-quality audio — a practically important property for real-world deployment. The theoretical underpinning of the SVM component is the RBF kernel K(x, y) = exp(−γ||x − y||²), where γ controls the influence radius of each training sample. The class_weight='balanced' strategy, applied to address class imbalance, weights the soft-margin penalty C proportionally to inverse class frequency, ensuring that minority classes such as lonely (25 samples) and scared (33 samples) in the present dataset receive equivalent gradient influence to the majority hungry class (397 samples) during optimization.

Despite these strengths, the classical ML paradigm exposes a structural limitation when confronted with multi-class cry reason classification on naturalistic datasets. Classic machine learning methods such as SVM and Random Forest, while achieving best performance on spectral features in controlled settings, are limited by the use of small datasets and constrained by the fact that features such as MFCC aggregate temporal information into fixed-dimensional statistics, discarding the sequential structure of the cry signal [9]. When audio features are summarized as means and standard deviations across an entire clip, the temporal dynamics of the cry — the onset trajectory, the pause-burst periodicity, the harmonic evolution over time — are irreversibly discarded. Two acoustically distinct cry types can produce nearly identical MFCC mean vectors while differing profoundly in their temporal evolution. This information destruction is not recoverable by any classifier operating downstream, regardless of its sophistication.

The present work's EDA confirmed this limitation quantitatively and independently. Nearest-neighbor purity analysis on 41-dimensional MFCC-derived features extracted from the Baby Cry Sense dataset yielded an overall cluster purity of 0.350 — barely above the 0.125 random baseline for eight classes — and PCA revealed that only 38.6% of feature variance is captured in two principal components, confirming near-complete linear inseparability in the handcrafted feature space. The most confused pairs — discomfort versus hungry (1,074 neighbor overlaps), hungry versus tired (976 overlaps), cold/hot versus hungry (899 overlaps) — directly predict the systematic misclassification patterns that any SVM-based Model A baseline will exhibit on this corpus. Özseven (2023) [8] conducted state-of-the-art research studying the extraction of infant cry signals and obtained the highest accuracy with hand-crafted features and the SVM classifier — yet even this optimized configuration operates within the ceiling imposed by the feature aggregation step itself. These findings collectively establish that the SVM baseline, while necessary as a rigorous probabilistic reference point, cannot alone constitute a deployable infant state recognition system. They motivate the transition to deep learning architectures capable of learning discriminative representations directly from the two-dimensional spectrogram without discarding temporal structure.

## Section 4 — Deep Learning for Audio Classification: From Spectrograms to Convolutional Networks

The reframing of audio classification as image classification through spectrogram representations constitutes the most consequential architectural shift in the infant cry analysis literature since the introduction of MFCC features. The key insight underlying this shift is that a Log-Mel Spectrogram — a two-dimensional matrix encoding energy distribution across mel-scaled frequency bands over time — preserves both the spectral structure and the temporal dynamics of an audio signal in a spatial format directly amenable to convolutional feature learning. Where MFCC aggregates temporal information into a single fixed-dimensional vector, the spectrogram retains the full time-frequency trajectory of the signal: the onset contour, the harmonic banding pattern, the pause-burst periodicity, and the spectral envelope evolution are all spatially encoded and therefore accessible to convolutional filters operating at multiple spatial scales simultaneously.

Abbaskhah et al. (2023) [2] directly demonstrated this superiority in a controlled three-way comparison, training SVM, MLP, and CNN classifiers on identical MFCC feature representations of five infant cry classes from the Dunstan Baby Language dataset — hungry, need to burp, tired, stomach cramp, and physical discomfort — using 19 MFCC coefficients and energy as the input feature vector, with performance evaluated across AUC-ROC, confusion matrix, accuracy, F1-score, recall, and precision. The results showed that the CNN-designed model achieved the best overall performance across all metrics, with the conclusion that performance improves by increasing the complexity of the model — directly confirming that the representational capacity of CNN architectures is better matched to the latent structure of infant cry signals than either linear SVM decision boundaries or the shallow non-linear mappings of MLP architectures.

The architectural explanation for this superiority lies in the inductive biases of convolutional operations. A standard convolutional layer applies a bank of learned filters across the input with weight sharing — the same filter detects the same local pattern regardless of its position in the spectrogram. This translation equivariance is precisely the right inductive bias for infant cry spectrograms: a harmonic stack occurring at time t=1.2s carries the same discriminative information as the same harmonic stack at t=2.8s, and a convolutional filter can detect both without learning two separate weights. Pooling layers further introduce a degree of translation invariance, making the learned representations robust to small temporal misalignments introduced by the variable onset timing of cry episodes across recordings. No equivalent mechanism exists in either SVM or MLP architectures operating on aggregate feature statistics.

However, the CNN architecture evaluated by Abbaskhah et al. (2023) [2] exposes a limitation that directly motivates the present work. The Dunstan dataset used consists of 315 audio files sampled at 44,100 Hz, confined to five cry classes reflecting a linguistically framed taxonomy of infant vocalization — a constrained, controlled corpus that does not reflect the acoustic heterogeneity of real-world home recording environments. The present work extends this paradigm to the Baby Cry Sense dataset — 1,126 audio files across eight cry categories, derived from crowdsourced mobile recordings at mixed sampling rates in uncontrolled acoustic environments — a substantially harder classification problem that better approximates the conditions under which a deployable infant state recognition system would operate. Furthermore, the standard CNN architecture, while demonstrating superior performance in server-side evaluation, presents a computational profile incompatible with microcontroller deployment: its parameter count and multiply-accumulate operation requirements exceed the memory envelope of the ESP32 target device. This limitation motivates the adoption of the Depthwise Separable CNN architecture in the present work, which factorizes each standard convolution into a depthwise spatial convolution followed by a pointwise channel-mixing convolution — reducing parameter count by a factor of eight to nine for typical 3×3 filters while preserving representational capacity sufficient for the eight-class classification task.

## Section 5 — Hybrid Approaches and Positioning of the Present Work

The limitations identified independently in both the classical ML and deep learning paradigms — feature aggregation discarding temporal dynamics in the former, computational infeasibility for edge deployment in the latter — converge toward a hybrid architectural resolution. The rationale for hybridization is not merely additive but synergistic: the probabilistic structure learned by classical ML models from compact handcrafted features provides a complementary representational basis to the spatially-learned patterns of convolutional networks, and their fusion can recover discriminative signal that neither component captures alone. The empirical case for this direction is established most directly by Hammoud et al. (2024) [3], whose work constitutes the closest published predecessor to the present system.

Hammoud et al. (2024) [3] apply time-, frequency-, and time-frequency-domain feature representations — zero-crossing rate and RMS in the time domain, Mel-spectrogram in the frequency domain, and MFCC in the time-frequency domain — alongside time-series imaging algorithms that transform 20 MFCC features into images using Gramian angular difference fields, Gramian angular summation fields, Markov transition fields, recurrence plots, and RGB GAF, which are then provided to machine learning classifiers including decision tree, random forest, KNN, and bagging, with the MFCC-based random forest classifier achieving 96.39% accuracy, outperforming the prior SOTA scalogram-based ShuffleNet classifier at 95.17%. The experimental foundation of this work is the Donate-A-Cry corpus [4] — the same dataset from which the Baby Cry Sense dataset used in the present work is directly derived — establishing a precise and verifiable performance baseline against which the present system's results can be compared.

Three specific limitations of Hammoud et al. (2024) [3] directly motivate the architectural choices of the present work. First, the proposed method classifies only five classes of infant crying — hunger, discomfort, belly pain, burping, and tiredness — leaving three categories present in the original Donate-A-Cry corpus unaddressed: loneliness, fear, and cold/hot states. The present system targets all eight categories, extending the classification scope to the full taxonomy of infant communicative states. Second, while Hammoud et al. (2024) [3] achieve strong accuracy through the random forest classifier, the random forest is an ensemble of decision trees operating on aggregate feature statistics — it does not learn spatial patterns from spectrogram images in the way a convolutional network does. The present work replaces this component with a Depthwise Separable CNN that operates directly on the Log-Mel Spectrogram, enabling the model to detect localized harmonic patterns, formant transitions, and temporal onset shapes that the random forest cannot access regardless of its ensemble size. Third, Hammoud et al. (2024) [3] note explicitly that their model performance might suffer when evaluated on longer signal durations, and identify the investigation of combining ZCR, RMS, and MFCCs in a single model as future work — a limitation that the present system addresses through its fixed-duration preprocessing pipeline with data-driven target duration selection, combined with multi-domain feature fusion in the hybrid architecture.

The positioning of the present work within the three-stage trajectory of the field is therefore precise. Liu et al. (2019) [1] established the ML baseline era, demonstrating that MFCC-based handcrafted features combined with classical classifiers constitute a rigorous but ceiling-bounded approach to cry language recognition. Abbaskhah et al. (2023) [2] proved empirically that CNN architectures outperform SVM and MLP on identical feature inputs, providing the quantitative justification for the transition to deep learning as the primary model tier. Hammoud et al. (2024) [3] represent the current state of the art on the Donate-A-Cry derived corpus, achieving 96.39% accuracy through a hybrid of time-series imaging and ensemble classifiers on five cry categories. The present system advances beyond each of these predecessors simultaneously: it targets eight cry categories rather than five, deploys a Depthwise Separable CNN rather than a random forest on spectrogram representations, introduces a preprocessing pipeline that explicitly controls for the dual-population sampling rate artifact identified in the EDA — an artifact unreported in any prior publication on this corpus — and evaluates the complete system under a rigorous three-model ablation framework (Model A: SVM baseline, Model B: DS-CNN only, Model C: Hybrid) designed to isolate and quantify the contribution of each architectural component independently.

## Section 6 — Depthwise Separable Convolutions and Efficient CNN Design

The adoption of Depthwise Separable Convolutions (DS-Conv) as the core computational primitive of Model B in the present work is grounded in a line of architectural research initiated by Howard et al. (2017) [12] in the MobileNet family of models. The fundamental contribution of MobileNet was to factorize the standard convolutional operation — which performs spatial filtering and cross-channel feature mixing simultaneously — into two sequential operations that separate these concerns. A depthwise convolution applies a single spatial filter of size D_K × D_K independently to each input channel without mixing across channels, and a pointwise convolution (a 1 × 1 convolution) subsequently combines the outputs of the depthwise step across channels to produce the desired number of output features. The arithmetic cost of a standard convolution with M input channels, N output channels, and D_F × D_F spatial output is D_K² · M · N · D_F² multiply-accumulate operations; the same effective mapping via DS factorisation requires D_K² · M · D_F² + M · N · D_F², yielding a reduction ratio of 1/N + 1/D_K². For the Model B architecture in the present work, which uses 3 × 3 depthwise filters and output channel dimensions up to 64, this ratio evaluates to 1/64 + 1/9 ≈ 0.127 — an approximate 8× reduction in arithmetic intensity without a proportional reduction in representational capacity. Howard et al. (2017) [12] demonstrated that MobileNet-V1 architectures achieve accuracy within 1–2 percentage points of full-cost VGG-style networks on ImageNet while requiring an order of magnitude fewer parameters and operations, establishing DS-Conv as the canonical primitive for parameter-efficient convolutional design.

The theoretical justification for DS-Conv extends beyond arithmetic efficiency to an argument about the statistical redundancy of standard convolutional filters. Chollet (2017) [13] advanced this argument most explicitly in the Xception architecture, framing DS-Conv as an extreme case of the Inception module's factorisation principle: if the cross-channel correlations and spatial correlations in convolutional feature maps are sufficiently decoupled, then the joint learning of both in a single convolutional kernel constitutes an inductive bias that is stronger than necessary and consumes parameters better allocated elsewhere. The empirical corollary is that DS-Conv networks, when given the same parameter budget as their standard-convolution counterparts, tend to reach higher accuracy because those parameters are concentrated in the pointwise mixing stage where cross-channel information integration occurs. Sandler et al. (2018) [14] subsequently introduced the MobileNetV2 architecture with inverted residual blocks, demonstrating that DS-Conv can be combined with expansion layers and skip connections to further improve accuracy at equivalent compute cost — a refinement that the present work does not adopt due to the modest scale of the eight-class classification task and the need to keep the parameter count well below the ESP32 memory envelope.

The present work's Model B applies DS-Conv in a manner directly adapted from MobileNet-V1 but simplified for the Log-Mel Spectrogram input shape (128 × 302 × 1) and eight-class output space. The architecture consists of an initial standard 3 × 3 convolution with 32 filters — included because the single-channel spectrogram input offers no cross-channel information for a depthwise operation to exploit — followed by three DS blocks producing feature maps of 64, 128, and 256 channels respectively, terminated by a global average pooling layer and a two-stage classification head. The total parameter count is 82,760, compared to 423,688 for an architecturally equivalent standard-convolution network (ablation variant B4 in the present work), representing a 5.1× reduction that is essential to the feasibility of the TensorFlow Lite Micro deployment target. Ablation experiment B4 provides direct empirical evidence that this reduction is not purchased at the cost of accuracy: the standard-convolution variant achieves 0.4177 Macro F1 on the 225-sample test set, while the DS-CNN shipped model achieves 0.4370 — a result consistent with the theoretical expectation from [12, 13] that DS-Conv networks can match or exceed standard architectures at substantially reduced parameter counts.

## Section 7 — Regularisation and Optimisation Theory for Small-Sample CNN Training

Training a CNN with 82,760 parameters on a training set of 974 examples presents a regime that the deep learning literature has studied extensively in the context of optimisation stability, initialisation sensitivity, and the trade-off between explicit regularisation and representational capacity. The initialisation scheme adopted throughout Model B is the He Normal distribution proposed by He et al. (2015) [15], which samples each weight from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with variance 2/n_in, where n_in is the fan-in of the layer. The derivation behind this choice — specifically that the ReLU activation function halves the expected variance of its input by zeroing the negative half-space — ensures that activation variance is preserved across layers during the initial forward pass, preventing the signal vanishing and gradient vanishing pathologies that afflicted earlier initialisation schemes such as Xavier/Glorot when combined with ReLU non-linearities. He et al. (2015) [15] showed that this initialisation is specifically necessary for deep networks with more than thirty convolutional layers to converge at all; although Model B at ten effective convolutional stages does not exhibit such severe sensitivity, the adoption of He Normal initialisation represents the principled default for any ReLU-based CNN and is applied uniformly to all Conv2D, DepthwiseConv2D, and Dense layers in the network.

The second theoretical pillar of Model B's training stability is Batch Normalization (BN), introduced by Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) [16]. BN normalizes the activations of each channel to zero mean and unit variance within a minibatch, then applies a learned per-channel affine transformation (γ, β) to restore representational flexibility. The original motivation for BN was the reduction of internal covariate shift — the phenomenon by which the distribution of each layer's inputs changes during training as the previous layers' weights update — but Santurkar et al. (2018) [17] subsequently demonstrated that the more important effect is the smoothing of the loss landscape, which allows higher learning rates and faster convergence without careful tuning of optimisation hyperparameters. The practical consequence for Model B is that every DepthwiseConv2D and pointwise Conv2D layer is followed by a BatchNormalization layer before the ReLU activation, and this BN layer provides sufficient implicit regularisation that explicit Dropout within the convolutional blocks becomes redundant — a hypothesis tested directly in the present work's ablation study. Ablation variant B3, which removes all Dropout layers from the model including the two in the classification head, achieves 0.4426 Macro F1, a value statistically indistinguishable from the full model's 0.4370 at the scale of this dataset, confirming that the convolutional blocks' regularisation requirement is adequately served by BN alone and that dropout within them would constitute destructive rather than corrective regularisation on the 974-sample training regime.

The loss function employed by Model B is categorical cross-entropy with label smoothing factor 0.1, following Szegedy et al. (2016) [18]. Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target vectors used in standard cross-entropy with softened distributions in which the correct class receives probability 1 − ε and the remaining probability ε is distributed uniformly across the other classes. This modification prevents the network from producing arbitrarily confident predictions — a pathology that arises when the softmax output approaches one-hot form and the logits must grow without bound to match — and thereby regularises the output distribution toward calibrated probabilities. In the context of the severe 15.9:1 class imbalance present in the Baby Cry Sense corpus, label smoothing provides a gentler and more gradient-stable form of imbalance handling than per-sample class weights, which were tested as ablation variant B1 and found to hurt performance by 0.053 Macro F1. The failure mode of per-sample class weights in this regime is mechanistically traceable to the ultra-small minority class sizes: with only five and seven test samples for the `lonely` and `scared` classes respectively, and correspondingly small training contingents dominated by augmented synthetic samples, the amplified loss contribution of each individual example causes gradient variance to increase beyond the point where stable optimisation is achievable. Label smoothing avoids this pathology because it operates at the level of the target distribution rather than the per-sample loss weighting, producing a uniform regularisation pressure that is independent of class frequency.

Finally, the training of Model B employs early stopping monitored on validation Macro F1 with patience of 25 epochs, and a ReduceLROnPlateau schedule that halves the Adam learning rate after ten epochs of stagnation on the validation loss. This combination of callbacks produces an adaptive training protocol that terminates automatically at the point of best validation performance and recovers the corresponding weights — a protocol adopted precisely to avoid the two failure modes that most commonly afflict small-dataset CNN training, namely premature early stopping on noisy single-seed validation curves and overfitting to the training distribution once the learning rate becomes too large for the remaining loss geometry. Prechelt (1998) [19] provides the foundational analysis of early stopping as an implicit regularisation mechanism equivalent to L2 weight decay under mild assumptions, establishing the theoretical basis for its routine use in modern CNN training pipelines.

## Section 8 — Interpretability: Grad-CAM for Audio Spectrogram Models

A central pillar of the Phase 2 evaluation rubric is technical validation through interpretability — the requirement that the learned CNN should not merely achieve high classification metrics but should be demonstrably attending to features that are causally related to the target phenomenon rather than to dataset artifacts or spurious correlations. The tool adopted for this purpose in Model B is Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), introduced by Selvaraju et al. (2017) [20]. Grad-CAM produces a class-discriminative localisation map by computing, for a given input x and target class c, the gradient of the class score y^c with respect to the feature maps A^k of a chosen convolutional layer. These gradients are spatially averaged to yield an importance weight α_k^c = (1/Z) Σ_i Σ_j ∂y^c/∂A_{ij}^k for each feature map k, and the weighted sum L^c = ReLU(Σ_k α_k^c · A^k) produces a two-dimensional attention map in the spatial coordinates of the chosen layer. The ReLU operation ensures that only features with positive influence on the target class contribute to the final heatmap, yielding a map that identifies exactly where in the input the model's confidence in the target class originates.

The application of Grad-CAM to audio classification via spectrograms represents a methodologically important translation of a technique developed originally for natural images. Hershey et al. (2017) [21], in their foundational analysis of CNN architectures for large-scale audio classification, framed this translation explicitly: when audio is represented as a two-dimensional time-frequency image via Log-Mel Spectrogram conversion, the entire apparatus of convolutional visualisation developed for vision — including Grad-CAM, saliency maps, and activation maximisation — becomes applicable without modification, provided the horizontal axis (time) and vertical axis (Mel-scaled frequency) are interpreted correctly in post-hoc analysis. This interpretability transfer is what permits the present work to verify, through the generated per-class heatmaps saved to `reports/gradcam_per_class.png`, that Model B attends to frequency regions consistent with the known acoustic properties of infant cries: the fundamental frequency band at 400–600 Hz corresponding to Mel bins 22–31, and the harmonic stack extending up to approximately 4,000 Hz corresponding to Mel bins near 100. The alternative failure case — in which the CNN learns to classify based on artifacts such as the zero-padded silence region introduced by the fixed 7.0 s duration preprocessing — would manifest as a concentration of Grad-CAM attention in time frames 190–302, and the quantitative silence-attention metric computed in the Phase 2 notebook directly tests for this pathology.

The specific layer targeted for Grad-CAM in Model B is the last DepthwiseConv2D layer before the final pointwise convolution and global average pooling, identified programmatically by the `get_last_conv_layer` helper in `src/model_dl.py`. This choice reflects a design consideration noted by Selvaraju et al. (2017) [20]: deeper convolutional layers exhibit higher-level, more class-discriminative features, but the last convolutional layer retains spatial resolution whereas post-pooling representations do not. For the Model B architecture with three successive MaxPool(2, 2) operations reducing the initial (128, 302) spatial grid to (16, 37), this choice yields heatmaps at 16 × 37 resolution that are subsequently upsampled via bilinear interpolation to the input dimensions (128, 302) for overlay visualisation on the original spectrogram. The empirical result of this interpretability analysis — that all eight classes exhibit greater than 60% content-region attention concentration — provides the visual evidence required by Pillar 4 of the evaluation rubric that Model B's GlobalAveragePooling head has successfully mitigated the silence-amplification failure mode (FM3) identified in Phase 1.

## Section 9 — TinyML and Edge Audio Inference

The deployment target for the complete Infant State Recognition System is the ESP32 microcontroller, a dual-core Tensilica processor with 512 KB of on-chip SRAM and no floating-point hardware accelerator — a platform representative of the emerging class of battery-powered edge devices on which audio classification must operate without network connectivity or cloud inference. The computational constraints of this class of device define the research area known as TinyML, systematically framed by Warden (2018) [22] through the introduction of the Speech Commands Dataset and the accompanying baseline micro-models that established the feasibility of real-time keyword spotting on 16-bit microcontrollers. The key insight that emerges from this foundational work is that the accuracy–efficiency trade-off curve for audio classification is substantially more forgiving than the analogous curve for vision tasks: the information-theoretic density of a short audio clip is considerably lower than that of a megapixel image, and a model with on the order of 10^5 parameters can achieve classification accuracy on constrained audio vocabularies comparable to what a 10^7 parameter model achieves on ImageNet-scale vision problems. Model B's parameter count of 82,760 is located exactly within this regime, and its 99.5 KB quantised TFLite footprint — achieved via the post-training dynamic-range quantisation pipeline described in Section 10 of the associated notebook — leaves approximately 400 KB of SRAM headroom for inference buffers, audio capture, and operating-system overhead on the ESP32.

The software infrastructure that enables this deployment is TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers (TFLite Micro), described in detail by David et al. (2021) [23]. TFLite Micro is a C++ interpreter-based inference runtime specifically engineered for microcontroller environments: it contains no dynamic memory allocation during inference, no operating-system dependencies, no filesystem access, and no floating-point operations when operating on integer-quantised models. The subset of TensorFlow operators supported by TFLite Micro is deliberately restricted to the operations that can be implemented deterministically and efficiently on 32-bit integer hardware, which constrains the set of architectures that can be deployed through this pipeline; Model B's constituent operations — Conv2D, DepthwiseConv2D, BatchNormalization (fused with the preceding convolution during TFLite conversion), ReLU, MaxPooling2D, GlobalAveragePooling2D, Dense, and Softmax — are all within this supported subset and translate without modification. Banbury et al. (2021) [24] subsequently introduced the MLPerf Tiny benchmark, which established canonical performance references for microcontroller audio classification models and provides the external comparison point against which the present work's Phase 3 inference latency measurements will be evaluated.

The quantisation strategy employed in the present work is post-training dynamic-range quantisation, which converts Float32 model weights to INT8 representation while retaining floating-point activations during inference. This scheme is adopted in preference to full INT8 quantisation — which would quantise both weights and activations — because dynamic-range quantisation requires no representative calibration dataset and incurs typically less than 1% accuracy degradation, whereas full INT8 quantisation can produce substantial accuracy drops on audio models when the activation distributions are poorly captured by the calibration set. Jacob et al. (2018) [25] provide the canonical analysis of quantisation-aware training and post-training quantisation trade-offs, and their findings directly inform the conservative choice of dynamic-range quantisation in Model B. The empirical verification of this choice — a 5 out of 5 prediction agreement between the original Keras model and the quantised TFLite interpreter on randomly selected test samples — establishes the correctness of the deployment pipeline as a prerequisite for the Phase 3 ESP32 integration.

## Section 10 — Phase 2 Positioning and Literature Synthesis

The four technical sections presented above ground each of Model B's architectural and training decisions in a specific line of published research. The architecture itself — three DS-Conv blocks with 32 → 64 → 128 → 256 channel progression, He Normal initialisation, BatchNorm regularisation, GlobalAveragePooling head, and two-stage Dense classifier — derives directly from the MobileNet lineage [12, 13, 14] adapted for single-channel spectrogram input. The training configuration — Adam optimizer, categorical cross-entropy with label smoothing, early stopping on validation Macro F1, ReduceLROnPlateau learning rate schedule — synthesizes the theoretical contributions of [15, 16, 17, 18, 19] into an adaptive protocol tuned for the 974-sample training regime. The interpretability validation — per-class Grad-CAM with content-versus-silence attention quantification — applies [20, 21] to the present domain of Log-Mel Spectrogram classification. The deployment pipeline — post-training dynamic-range quantisation to TFLite Micro with a 512 KB SRAM target — operationalises the TinyML research program of [22, 23, 24, 25] for the specific eight-class infant cry classification task.

The contribution of Model B to the trajectory established in Sections 1 through 5 is therefore both empirically demonstrable and theoretically principled. Empirically, Model B's test-set Macro F1 of 0.4370 constitutes an 8.5% relative improvement over Model A's Phase 1 SVM baseline of 0.4029 on an identical 225-sample evaluation partition — a gain that is attributable, through the ablation studies B1 through B4, to specific architectural mechanisms rather than to training-set-dependent artifacts. Theoretically, each of the three Phase 1 failure modes identified in the SVM baseline receives a principled architectural response: translation sensitivity (FM1) is addressed by the translation equivariance of 2D convolution combined with the translation invariance induced by MaxPooling; temporal blindness (FM2) is addressed by the joint spatial-temporal receptive fields of 3×3 filters operating on the time-frequency plane; and silence amplification (FM3) is addressed by the GlobalAveragePooling operation, which reduces the contribution of zero-padded frames to near-zero regardless of the magnitude of their input features. The Phase 3 hybrid system described in the project's repository documentation constitutes the final integration step in this research programme: by fusing the SVM decision-function outputs with the DS-CNN's 256-dimensional GAP features, Model C is designed to inherit the complementary strengths of both paradigms and to recover the discriminative signal — most prominently the `discomfort ↔️ hungry` collapse — that neither Model A nor Model B can resolve in isolation.
